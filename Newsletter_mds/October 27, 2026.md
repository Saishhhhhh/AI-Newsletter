## 📄 Papers Worth Reading
- **[ReGE: Stronger Agents through Experience Synthesis](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.33861)** by Akshat Gupta et al. — ReGE is a framework for turning recorded experience into guidance for a fresh attempt at a task. Its main implementation, ReGE-Syn, uses a single coach call to jointly synthesize bounded execution records and available artifacts into self-contained guidance. This approach has shown significant improvements in accuracy across four benchmarks.
- **[CRM-Agent-Bench: Benchmarking Tool-Using Agents in Stateful Enterprise Environments](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.32004)** by Salesforce Misc et al. — CRM-Agent-Bench is an execution-grounded benchmark of 555 stateful, multi-turn tasks across nine business domains and 36 capability tags. Each task supplies a task-specific executable contract, and success is the strict conjunction of the checks declared for that task. The benchmark offers an auditable framework for measuring contract-grounded business-transaction integrity and adherence to task-declared benchmark policies.
- **[Gluing Local Contexts into Global Meaning: A Sheaf-Theoretic Decomposition of Transformer Representations](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.17117)** by CWru-Aism et al. — This paper treats each paraphrase pair as a local witness that two activations should agree and builds a cellular sheaf over the paraphrase graph. The Laplacian L_F answers the question on a frozen model with no training, and the kernel H^0 is a content subspace. The paper demonstrates how this approach can be used to improve the interpretability of transformer representations.

## 🎓 One Thing to Learn
One concept that's gaining traction in the AI community is the use of sheaf theory to decompose transformer representations. Sheaf theory is a mathematical framework that allows us to study the relationships between different parts of a system. In the context of transformer representations, sheaf theory can be used to identify the content subspace, which is the part of the representation that remains unchanged under paraphrasing. This has significant implications for our understanding of how transformers work and how we can improve their interpretability. By applying sheaf theory, we can better understand how transformers capture meaning and how we can use this knowledge to improve their performance. Sheaf theory can also be applied to other areas of AI, such as natural language processing and computer vision, to improve the accuracy and efficiency of AI models.
